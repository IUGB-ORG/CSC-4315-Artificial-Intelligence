On Friday an array became a thing with a shape, and .shape became the first thing you print. Today that pays off twice: once because broadcasting is a rule about shapes and nothing else, and once because the nastiest bug in NumPy is a shape you did not notice.

Part 1 · 18 minutes

Broadcasting — two rules, and you can predict every result

The most powerful and most dangerous feature in NumPy. It is also, mercifully, completely mechanical.

You have already used it. a + 10 adds a single number to a whole array — the 10 was broadcast across every element. The general rule extends that to arrays of different shapes.

The algorithm — apply it in this order, every time

1. Line the shapes up from the right. If one is shorter, pad it on the left with 1s.

2. In each column, the two sizes must be equal, or one of them must be 1. A 1 is stretched to match. Anything else is an error.

Line up from the right — three worked cases
Shape AShape BResultWhy
(3, 4)(4,)(3, 4)Padded to (1,4); the 1 stretches to 3.
(3, 4)(3, 1)(3, 4)Explicit 1 stretches to 4.
(3, 1)(1, 4)(3, 4)Both stretch. This is the one that surprises people.
(3, 4)(3,)ERRORPadded to (1,3). Rightmost column: 4 vs 3. No.
(3,)(4,)ERROR3 vs 4, neither is 1.
the error you will see more than any other this semestererror
np.array([1,2,3]) + np.array([1,2,3,4])

ValueError: operands could not be broadcast together
            with shapes (3,) (4,)

Read it as a gift. NumPy has told you both shapes and where they disagree. Nine times in ten the fix is a .reshape(-1, 1) or a transpose, and the diagnosis takes ten seconds once you trust the message.

Part 2 · 10 minutes · the important part of today

The bug that does not raise an error

An error is a good day. This is the other kind.

You have three predictions and three true values. You want the mean squared error. The predictions came out of one calculation and the targets came from a column of a table — a completely ordinary situation, and one you will be in during session 10.

predict the output. do not scroll.python
p = np.array([1., 2., 3.])          # predictions       shape (3,)
t = np.array([[1.], [2.], [3.]])     # targets           shape (3, 1)

mse = ((p - t) ** 2).mean()
print(mse)
The predictions are exactly correct. What does it print?

Every prediction matches its target perfectly. Commit to an answer.

It prints 1.3333333333333333. The predictions are perfect and the loss is not zero.

Why. p is (3,), padded to (1, 3). t is (3, 1). Both have a 1, so both stretch — and the result is a (3, 3) matrix of every prediction minus every target:

[[ 0.  1.  2.]
 [-1.  0.  1.]
 [-2. -1.  0.]]

The diagonal is the answer you wanted — all zeros. The other six entries are comparisons between unrelated pairs, and the mean is taken over all nine. NumPy did nothing wrong. Row three of the broadcasting table says (1,3) and (3,1) combine to (3,3), and that is exactly what happened.

The fix — either one:

((p - t.ravel()) ** 2).mean()  # 0.0 — flatten t to (3,)
((p[:, None] - t) ** 2).mean() # 0.0 — lift p to (3, 1)

Both give 0.0. The rule is to make the two shapes agree before the arithmetic, not to hope.

🚨
Why I am spending ten minutes on one bug

A model that will not train is usually this. Your loss is a number, so nothing crashes; it goes down, so nothing looks wrong; it plateaus somewhere useless and you spend two days changing the learning rate. In session 16 several of you will hit exactly this, and I would rather you recognise the smell than rediscover it.

The habit: print .shape on both sides before any operation you are unsure of. It costs one line and it is the single most effective debugging technique in this entire course.

Part 3 · 16 minutes

Three plots, and no more than three

Matplotlib is enormous. This course uses a small corner of it, and you will use that corner in almost every session from Friday to December.

The reason to learn this today rather than later is simple: from session 9 onward, nearly every claim in this course is a claim about a curve. A loss that goes down. A learning rate that is too large. A model that fits the training data and not the test data. Describing those in words is a poor substitute for looking at them, and looking at them costs three lines.

the import, and the only two lines that always appearpython
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

x = np.linspace(-3, 3, 200)   # 200 evenly spaced numbers from -3 to 3
y = x ** 2

plt.plot(x, y)
plt.show()

np.linspace(a, b, n) is the function you will reach for constantly: n evenly spaced values from a to b, endpoints included. Compare it with the comprehension you wrote last Tuesday — [i/10 for i in range(-30, 31)] — and you have the whole argument for NumPy in one line again.

Plot one — the line, for anything that changes

this is the plot you will draw most often all termpython
epochs = np.arange(1, 21)
loss   = 2.0 * np.exp(-0.2 * epochs)     # a plausible training curve

plt.plot(epochs, loss, marker="o")
plt.xlabel("epoch")
plt.ylabel("loss")
plt.title("training loss")
plt.grid(True)
plt.show()
🏷️
An unlabelled axis is a wrong plot

In this course a figure without xlabel and ylabel is incomplete, and in the midterm it loses marks. This is not fussiness. A loss curve and an accuracy curve look identical and mean opposite things — one going up is good news and the other is a disaster. The label is what carries the meaning.

Plot two — the scatter, for data points

every dataset in unit 2 gets looked at this way firstpython
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)          # seed 0 — everyone gets the same points
hours  = rng.uniform(0, 10, 30)
scores = 8 * hours + rng.normal(0, 6, 30)   # a trend, plus noise

plt.scatter(hours, scores)
plt.xlabel("hours studied")
plt.ylabel("exam score")
plt.show()

Thirty points, an obvious upward trend, and no line through them yet. In session 10 you will put that line there twice — once solved exactly, once learned by descent — and this scatter is the picture you will put it on.

Plot three — two things on one pair of axes

the comparison plot: data against model, or train against testpython
plt.scatter(hours, scores, label="data")
plt.plot(hours, 8 * hours, color="red", label="model")
plt.xlabel("hours studied")
plt.ylabel("exam score")
plt.legend()
plt.show()

Two calls before one show put both on the same axes. Add label= to each and call legend(), or the reader has to guess which is which. In session 22 this exact plot — two curves, one legend — is how overfitting is diagnosed.

🧭
That is the whole of it

plot, scatter, labels, legend, show. There is a great deal more in Matplotlib and you will not need it. When you eventually do — a second panel, a log axis — look it up then, having a reason.

Part 4 · 6 minutes

Everything so far, in six lines

An array, a broadcast, and a picture. This is a rehearsal for session 9, where the same six lines draw a loss surface and you find its lowest point by hand.

run this — it is session 9 in miniaturepython
w = np.linspace(-2, 4, 200)      # 200 candidate values of a weight
loss = (w - 1.5) ** 2 + 0.5       # the error each one would give

plt.plot(w, loss)
plt.xlabel("weight")
plt.ylabel("loss")
plt.show()

print(w[loss.argmin()])          # 1.497... — the best weight, found by looking

argmin gives the position of the smallest value, which you then use to index back into w. Notice what happened: you found the best weight by trying two hundred of them and picking the winner. That works here because there is one weight. In session 13 there are thirteen, and in a real network there are millions — which is precisely why session 9 exists.
