Part 2 · 15 minutes

The answer that sounds right and is not

There is a tempting one-line definition of AI. It is popular, it is repeated in textbooks, and it falls apart on the second example you throw at it.

The tempting definition

Ask most people to define artificial intelligence and, after some hedging, you get something close to this:

The definition we are about to reject

“It is AI if a human did not write the rules — if the machine worked them out for itself.”

It is an attractive test. It is crisp, it is easy to apply, and it correctly rejects the thermostat and the calculator. A human wrote if temperature > 26 then switch on; a human wrote the square-root algorithm. Neither machine worked anything out. Good so far.

Now apply it to Deep Blue.

Every rule inside Deep Blue was written by a human being. The rules of chess, hand-coded. The evaluation function that scored a position — material, king safety, pawn structure — hand-tuned by grandmasters. The search procedure, hand-written. Deep Blue learned nothing. It did not improve between games. Switch it off and on and it is exactly the machine it was before.

So by the tempting definition, Deep Blue is not artificial intelligence. And yet it did the thing that, for forty years, everyone had agreed would be the proof that machines could think: it beat Garry Kasparov.

🛑
Where the definition breaks

“Did a human write the rules?” is not a definition of artificial intelligence. It is a definition of machine learning — which is one branch of AI, not the whole of it. Treating the two as the same thing quietly deletes forty years of the field, and it will make the rest of this course incoherent.

This is worth stating plainly because it is the single most common confusion in the room, including among people who work with these systems every day. In 2026 the words AI and machine learning are used interchangeably in press releases, job adverts and, frequently, in conference talks. They are not the same, and you will spend the rest of this semester on the difference.

Part 3 · 20 minutes

Two questions, asked in order

One question was never going to be enough. Two, asked in the right sequence, will carry you through the entire field.

The test — use this for the rest of the semester

Question 1 — Does the task require intelligence at all?
If a human doing this task would be said to be thinking — perceiving, reasoning, planning, judging, using language — then the task is a candidate. If a human doing it would just be following a fixed procedure, it is not.

Question 2 — Where did the rules come from?
Only ask this if question 1 came back yes. Did a person write the rules down, or did the machine extract them from data? This does not decide whether it is AI. It decides what kind.

The order matters. Question 1 is the gate: it separates artificial intelligence from ordinary computation. Question 2 is the classifier: it tells you which of three families you are looking at, and therefore what you should expect from the system, how it fails, and what it would take to improve it.

Question 1, applied

A thermostat compares two numbers. A human doing that is not thinking, they are comparing two numbers. Not AI — and notice we never had to ask who wrote the rule.

A calculator executes an algorithm that is exact, known, and finite. A human doing long division is not exercising judgement. Not AI

Playing chess well, recognising which email is unwanted, finding a good route through a city you do not know, writing coherent prose about the cocoa trade — a human doing any of these would be described as thinking. All four pass question 1

⚖️
Question 1 is a judgement call, and that is acceptable

There is no bright line, and philosophers have argued about it for seventy years. You will meet genuinely borderline cases — is optical character recognition intelligence, or is it pattern matching that stopped being impressive? The test is still useful. A test that gives you a clear answer on ninety per cent of cases and an honest argument on the rest is worth far more than a crisp rule that gives you confidently wrong answers.

Question 2, applied — the three paradigms

Of the systems that passed question 1, ask where the rules came from. You get three distinct families. We will spend all of session 2 on these; today, just meet them.

Three paradigms — the same field, three different machines

All three are artificial intelligence. All three pass question 1. They differ only in the answer to question 2 — and that difference determines everything practical about them.

Part 4 · 15 minutes

Back to the six systems

Now with the test in hand. Compare these against the answers you wrote at the start.

Not AI
A · The thermostat   ·   E · The calculator

Both fail at question 1.

Comparing two numbers is not thinking; computing a square root by a known exact algorithm is not thinking. We never reach question 2, and it is worth noticing that we did not need to — asking "did a human write the rule?" first would have given the right answer here for entirely the wrong reason.

A caveat you should be able to make yourself: a modern learning thermostat, which builds a model of when you are home and what you like, does pass question 1. The device on the wall in 1970 does not. Same word, different machine.

AISearch-based
B · Deep Blue   ·   D · Google Maps routing

Pass question 1. On question 2: humans wrote every rule.

These two are the same machine wearing different clothes, and this is why D split the room. Both take a problem with an astronomical number of possible answers, and both search it far faster and far deeper than a person could — Deep Blue through chess positions, Maps through street networks. Neither learned anything. Both are unambiguously AI, and search is one of the oldest and most successful branches of the field.

If you said yes to Deep Blue and no to Google Maps, you were not being inconsistent — you were responding to how impressive each one feels. That instinct is worth naming, because it is the source of a long-running joke in this field: as soon as something works reliably, people stop calling it AI. Routing, spell-check, optical character recognition and speech transcription were all once flagship AI results. Now they are just software.

AILearning-based
C · The spam filter   ·   F · ChatGPT

Pass question 1. On question 2: the machine extracted the rules from data.

Nobody wrote a rule saying that the word "lottery" plus an unfamiliar sender plus five exclamation marks means spam. The filter found that pattern in a million labelled examples. Nobody wrote a rule about the cocoa trade either. The scale differs by a factor of a billion; the principle is identical.

This third family is what the rest of CSC 4315 is about — not because the other two are unimportant, but because this is the one you can build from scratch in a semester, and the one that has changed since 2012.

The point of the whole exercise

The six systems sort into three groups, not two. Anyone who tells you that AI means "the machine learned it" has handed you a test that classifies Deep Blue, Google Maps and every expert system as not-AI — which is a strange thing to say about the programs that carried the field for its first forty years.

Not synonyms — nested boxes

One more piece of vocabulary before we finish, because these five words are used interchangeably in the press and they are not interchangeable at all.

Each term sits inside the one before it

Read it outward and each sentence is true: every large language model is deep learning, all deep learning is machine learning, all machine learning is AI. Read it inward and every sentence is false. Deep Blue lives in the outermost box only. Your spam filter lives in the second box but not the third.

Part 5 · 10 minutes

What you will build between now and December

This course has an unusual rule, and it is the reason it is organised the way it is.

The rule of this course

You will not call a library function until you have written the thing it replaces.

By session 14 you will have built a neural network in NumPy — the forward pass by hand on paper, then in code; the gradients derived on the board, then coded, then verified numerically. Only in session 17 will you be allowed to write model.fit(). When you do, you will be able to name every line of your own code that the call replaced.

  • 1
    Sessions 1–3 · What AI is

    Today's test, the three paradigms, eighty years of history including two collapses, and an honest account of where the field actually stands in 2026.

  • 2
    Sessions 4–9 · The tools you need and nothing more

    Two sessions of Python, two of NumPy, the small amount of linear algebra a network actually uses, and gradient descent worked out with pencil and paper before any code. Six sessions, deliberately unhurried.

  • 3
    Sessions 10–14 · A neural network from nothing

    No libraries. Least squares, one neuron, the XOR wall, a forward pass you compute by hand, and backpropagation you derive yourself. The heart of the course.

  • 4
    Sessions 16–19 · What the libraries were doing all along

    Your two hundred lines become four, in scikit-learn, PyTorch and Keras, shown side by side against your own code.

  • 5
    Sessions 20–23 · Data that fights back

    Missing values, leakage, class imbalance, overfitting, and images at a scale where the tricks matter.

  • 6
    Sessions 24–26 · Language, and a model you adapt

    Tokens, embeddings, attention computed by hand on three words — and then a small language model, already trained, that you fine-tune on a corpus of your choosing and watch change voice.

  • 7
    Sessions 27–28 · Getting real work out of frontier models

    Prompting as an engineering discipline, then agents, tools and retrieval. Placed at the end deliberately: this material reads completely differently once you have taken a model apart yourself and put one back to work.
