Part 1 · 10 minutes

Slope: which way is down, and how steeply

This is all the calculus this course needs, and you may already have it.

In Lab 1 you plotted f(x) = x². Look at it again. At any point on that curve there is a slope — how fast the curve rises as you move right. The slope is written f′(x), and for this function it is:

The one derivative you must know today

f(x) = x²   ⟹   f′(x) = 2x

At x = 2 the slope is 4: steep, going up to the right. At x = −2 the slope is −4: steep, going down to the right. At x = 0 the slope is 0, and that is the bottom.

The sign of the slope points away from the minimum — always

Notice the pattern, because the whole method rests on it. Where the slope is positive, the minimum is to the left. Where the slope is negative, the minimum is to the right. In both cases, you should move in the direction opposite to the sign of the slope.

Part 2 · 6 minutes

The rule, in one line

Every model in this course, and every frontier model in the world, is trained by repeating this.

Gradient descent

x ← x − η · f′(x)

Take the slope. Move against it. The Greek letter η (eta) is the learning rate — how big a step to take. It is a number you choose, and Part 5 is about how much that choice matters.

The minus sign is the whole idea: it makes the step oppose the slope, so you always move downhill. And the size of the step is proportional to the slope, which gives you something valuable for free — the steps get smaller as you approach the bottom, because the slope gets smaller. You do not have to slow down manually. The method slows itself down.

🌫️
Why not just solve f′(x) = 0?

For x² you could, and in session 10 you will do exactly that for a straight-line fit. But for a network with nine weights — let alone nine billion — there is no formula to solve. Gradient descent needs only the slope where you currently stand. It is the method that survives when the analytic answer does not exist, which is why it is the one that scaled.

Three things to notice in your own table

f(x) falls every single step — 4.0, 2.56, 1.64, 1.05, 0.67, 0.43. That column is what a loss curve is.

The steps shrink — 0.4, then 0.32, then 0.256. Nobody told it to slow down; the slope did that.

It never quite arrives. After five steps x = 0.655, not 0. It approaches 0 and never reaches it. Training does not finish; you stop it. Session 16 is about deciding when.

Part 5 · 16 minutes · the heart of the session

The learning rate decides everything

And for this function, exactly one number tells you what will happen. You can compute it in your head.

Keep the rule general and see what one step does:

substitute f′(x) = 2x, but leave η as a symbolarithmetic
x ← x - η·2x  =  (1 - 2η)·x
Every step multiplies x by the same factor

factor = 1 − 2η

So the behaviour depends only on |1 − 2η|. Below 1, x shrinks toward zero. Above 1, it grows. Equal to 1, it does neither, forever. Negative, it flips sides each step.

You will not get a formula this clean again in this course. Use it now to build intuition you will keep.

Three questions about that table

Answer all three before opening. The second one catches almost everyone.

Q1. Why is η = 0.5 exact? Because 1 − 2(0.5) = 0, so x is multiplied by zero. The step happens to be precisely the distance to the minimum. This is a coincidence of this function — do not expect it again, and never go looking for the "perfect" learning rate on a real problem.

Q2. η = 0.1 and η = 0.9 reach 0.336 after the same number of steps. Are they equally good? The magnitudes match exactly — both factors have size 0.80 — so on this function they converge at an identical rate. But 0.9 is far more dangerous. It sits close to the cliff at η = 1.0; a slightly steeper function, or one more layer, and it diverges. 0.1 has room. In practice you choose the stable side of equally-fast options, because you are never as certain about the curvature as you are here.

Q3. What is actually wrong with η = 0.01? Nothing, except time. It converges — 0.98 is below 1 — but after eight steps it has moved from 2.000 to 1.702. It would need roughly 340 steps to reach where η = 0.1 gets in 8. Multiply that by a network that takes a minute per pass and you have wasted a week to avoid a risk that was not there.

💥
What divergence looks like when it happens to you

Look at the η = 1.1 row: 2.4, 2.88, 3.46, 4.15, 4.98… growing by 20% each step. In session 16 this will appear on your screen as a loss that climbs instead of falling, and then prints nan. When you see nan, your first move is to divide the learning rate by ten. Not to redesign the network. Not to get more data. Divide by ten and run it again.

Part 6 · 8 minutes

The chain rule — the second and last piece of calculus

You need it because a network is functions inside functions, and session 14 is nothing but this rule applied carefully.

Suppose a quantity depends on u, and u depends on x. How fast does the quantity change as x changes? You multiply the two rates.

The chain rule

dy/dx = (dy/du) × (du/dx)

If u changes 3 times as fast as x, and y changes 5 times as fast as u, then y changes 15 times as fast as x. That is the entire content of the rule.

worked, on a function you will meet again in session 11arithmetic
f(x) = (x - 3)²

# name the inside
u = x - 3            du/dx = 1
f = u²               df/du = 2u

# multiply the rates
df/dx = 2u × 1 = 2(x - 3)

# check at x = 0:   2(0-3) = -6   → slope is negative, minimum is to the right ✓
# check at x = 5:   2(5-3) = +4   → slope is positive, minimum is to the left  ✓
🔬
You can always check a derivative without trusting your algebra

Nudge x by a tiny amount and measure how much f moved:

h = 1e-6
numerical = (f(x + h) - f(x - h)) / (2 * h)

For f(x) = (x−3)² at x = 0 this gives −6.0000, matching the algebra to four decimals. This is called a numerical gradient check, and in session 14 it is how you will prove your hand-derived backpropagation is correct before you trust it. Remember that it exists.

Why a network needs it

Your 2–3–1 network from Tuesday is a chain: the input produces hidden values, the hidden values produce an output, the output produces a loss. To know how much a weight in W1 affects the loss, you must multiply the rates along that chain. That is what backpropagation is — the chain rule, applied layer by layer, backwards. You now have the tool. Session 14 does the work.

Two things I have not told you the truth about yet

🕳️

Real surfaces are not bowls

x² has one minimum and you always find it. A real loss surface has many valleys, and gradient descent finds whichever one it happens to roll into. In practice this matters far less than it sounds — but it is why two identical training runs can end at different answers, and why session 16 asks you to fix a random seed.

🧭

Real problems have many dimensions

You descended one number. A network has thousands, and the slope becomes a gradient — one slope per parameter, all computed at once. The update rule is unchanged; it just applies to every entry of W simultaneously. NumPy does that in one line, which is the reason we spent two sessions on arrays.
