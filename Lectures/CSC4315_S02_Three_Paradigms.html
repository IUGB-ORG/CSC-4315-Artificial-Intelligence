Where we left off

Two questions, asked in order. Does the task require intelligence at all? If yes: where did the rules come from? The first question separates AI from ordinary computation. The second sorts what is left into three families.

Today we take those three families seriously — not as a tidy classification scheme, but as three genuine research programmes that each dominated the field for a period, each produced systems that worked, and each ran into a wall that the next one climbed over. You cannot understand why deep learning looks the way it does without knowing what it was reacting against.

The claim of this session

These are not three levels of sophistication, with learning at the top. They are three different answers to one question: how does knowledge get into the machine? Each answer has a cost, and the cost is what determines which one you should reach for.

Paradigm 1 · 20 minutes

Rules-based: ask the expert, write it down

The dominant approach from the mid-1970s to the late 1980s, and the most commercially successful period the field had known up to that point.

The idea is disarmingly simple. Intelligence in a specialist domain looks, from the outside, like a large number of learned associations. A doctor sees a set of symptoms and reaches a diagnosis. An engineer sees an order and knows which cable it needs. If you could get those associations out of the expert's head and into a file, the machine could apply them faster and more consistently than the expert ever could — and it would never be tired, distracted, or on holiday.

So you sit with the expert. You ask them what they do. You write it down as rules.

What a rule actually looks like

MYCIN was built at Stanford in the early 1970s to diagnose bacterial blood infections and recommend antibiotics. Its knowledge base held roughly 500 to 600 rules of this shape:

a MYCIN-style production rulerule base
IF    the stain of the organism is gram-negative
AND   the morphology of the organism is rod
AND   the aerobicity of the organism is anaerobic
THEN  there is suggestive evidence (0.6)
      that the identity of the organism is Bacteroides

Three things are worth noticing, because all three come back later in the course.

  • 1
    It is completely transparent

    You can read it. A doctor can read it, disagree with it, and tell you exactly which clause is wrong. Nothing in this course after session 13 will have that property, and you should feel the loss when it happens.

  • 2
    It carries a number — 0.6 — that a human chose

    MYCIN called these certainty factors. Somebody decided that this combination of evidence was worth 0.6 and not 0.7. Hold that thought: in session 13 you will meet a system with nine such numbers, and in session 26 one with millions — and none of them will be chosen by a person.

  • 3
    It only fires on exactly what it was given

    Gram-negative, rod, anaerobic. Nothing else. A case that is nearly this gets nothing at all. There is no notion of similarity anywhere in the system.

Did it work? Yes — and that is the uncomfortable part

🧬

MYCIN · Stanford, 1970s

In a blind evaluation, a panel of eight independent specialists judged MYCIN's therapy recommendations acceptable 65% of the time. Five Stanford faculty members scored between 42.5% and 62.5% on the same cases. The machine was competitive with the humans who taught it.

🖥️

XCON / R1 · DEC, 1980

Configured VAX computer orders. About 2,500 rules. By 1986 it had processed 80,000 orders at 95–98% accuracy and was estimated to be saving Digital Equipment Corporation around $25 million a year. Order fulfilment fell from weeks to days.

These were not laboratory toys. XCON is the reason the phrase expert system appeared in annual reports. An entire industry formed around building them, and for most of the 1980s that industry was what the words "artificial intelligence" meant commercially.

The two walls

🧱
Wall one — the knowledge acquisition bottleneck

Experts cannot tell you what they know. A radiologist can look at a scan and say "that is wrong" in half a second and then spend twenty minutes failing to explain which pixels made them say it. The knowledge you most want is precisely the knowledge that never made it into words. Every rule-based system is capped, permanently, at what the expert could articulate — and that is a small fraction of what the expert can do.

🧱
Wall two — rules interact, and the interactions grow faster than the rules

XCON started around 2,500 rules. By 1989 a successor system had grown to roughly 17,000 rules covering 31,000 components. Add rule 9,000 and it may silently contradict rule 300, in a case nobody has tested. Parts catalogues change by up to a third a year, so the rules never stop needing revision. The maintenance cost does not grow with the number of rules; it grows with the number of ways they can interact.

MYCIN was never used on a patient. Not because it did not work — the evaluation says it worked. It was never deployed because of unresolved questions about who is responsible when a program gives the wrong advice, and because entering a case took a physician more than half an hour. That is a lesson about deployment that has nothing to do with accuracy, and it is at least as relevant in 2026 as it was in 1976.

When the expert-system market collapsed at the end of the 1980s, it did not collapse because the systems stopped working. It collapsed because they cost more to maintain than they saved. We will return to this in session 3 as the second AI winter.

Paradigm 2 · 25 minutes

Search-based: do not encode the answer, encode the goal

Older than expert systems, and still running underneath a great deal of software you used this morning.

Search takes a different bet. Do not try to write down what a good move is. Write down what the legal moves are, write down how to recognise a good outcome, and then let the machine look ahead far enough to find the path that gets there.

This turns intelligence into a resource problem, and that is its great appeal: resources get cheaper every year, and expert patience does not.

Why it is hard: the numbers

Consider chess. From a typical position there are around 35 legal moves. Your opponent then has around 35 replies. A full game runs about 80 half-moves.

The branching problem — why you cannot simply look at everything

That last number is Shannon's 1950 estimate of the chess game tree. It is not large in the way a big number is large. There is no computer, now or ever, that will examine 10120 anything. Brute force in the literal sense is off the table permanently.

So search-based AI is not really about searching everything. It is about three tricks for searching almost nothing and still being right:

  • 1
    Stop early and guess

    Look ahead a fixed depth, then score the position with an evaluation function instead of playing to the end. This is where all the human knowledge hides.

  • 2
    Assume the opponent plays well — minimax

    You pick the move that maximises your score; your opponent picks the reply that minimises it. Alternate. This is the algorithm you will run by hand in two minutes.

  • 3
    Prove whole branches irrelevant — alpha-beta pruning

    Often you can show a branch cannot possibly affect the answer without examining it. In the best case this lets you search twice as deep for the same effort.

Do this now — minimax on paper, two minutes

You are MAX, at the root. It is your move; you choose among three. Your opponent MIN then chooses among three replies. The nine numbers at the bottom are the scores of the resulting positions, from your point of view — bigger is better for you.

Work upward from the leaves. Which move should MAX play?
Worked answer

Try it before opening this. Most people get it wrong the first time, and the reason they get it wrong is the interesting part.

Left MIN node: chooses the smallest of 3, 12, 8 → 3.
Middle MIN node: smallest of 2, 4, 6 → 2.
Right MIN node: smallest of 14, 5, 6 → 5.

Root MAX: largest of 3, 2, 5 → 5. MAX plays move C.

Now the trap. The single best number on the board is 14, and it sits under move C — but you will never receive it, because after you play C your opponent simply plays the reply worth 5. And the 12 under move A is equally unreachable. You do not get to choose the leaf. You only choose the subtree. That one sentence is what minimax is.

And now pruning. Suppose you evaluate left to right. After the left MIN node you know you can guarantee at least 3. You then look at the middle node's first leaf and see 2. MIN will end up with at most 2 there — you already know it can't beat 3, so the remaining two leaves under move B can never change your decision. Skip them. Two of nine leaves never examined, with no loss of correctness. That is alpha-beta pruning, and at scale it is the difference between searching six moves ahead and twelve.

Deep Blue, and the part of the story that is usually left out

In May 1997 Deep Blue beat the reigning world champion Garry Kasparov in a six-game match. The headline figures are genuinely astonishing:

Deep Blue, 1997
Positions evaluated per secondaround 200 million
Kasparov, for comparisonroughly 5
Hardware30 processor nodes driving 480 custom chess chips
Typical search depthsix to twelve half-moves, up to forty in forced lines
Terms in the evaluation functionabout 8,000

That last row is the one to sit with. Eight thousand features — passed pawns, bishop pairs, king safety scaled by the material left on the board — each designed by a human being and each carrying a weight that a human chose. The team has said that between the 1996 and 1997 matches, the great majority of their effort went into designing, testing and tuning that evaluation function, with a grandmaster on the team to advise. They also built automated tools to help tune the weights, and one of those tools found that the hand-chosen king-safety weights had been systematically too low.

Apply the two-question test to Deep Blue

Question 1: playing world-class chess plainly requires intelligence when a human does it. passes

Question 2: every rule came from a human. The legal moves, the search procedure, and all eight thousand evaluation terms. Deep Blue learned nothing, and was exactly the same machine after the match as before it.

So it is AI, and it is not machine learning. If your definition of AI cannot accommodate that sentence, your definition is broken.

🗺️
The same paradigm, in your pocket

When you ask for the fastest route from Grand-Bassam to Abidjan, the software builds a graph of intersections and roads, assigns each road a cost from live traffic, and runs a shortest-path search over it. No learning is involved in the routing itself. This is search-based AI, working perfectly, several million times a day — and almost nobody calls it AI any more, which is exactly the effect we noticed in session 1.

The wall search hit

Search needs two things the world often refuses to supply: a complete set of legal moves, and a scoring function for a position. Chess has both by definition. Route-finding has both.

Now ask what the legal moves are for "is there a cat in this photograph," or "is this email spam," or "what is the next word in this sentence." There are none. There is no game tree. There is nothing to search. Search-based AI was not defeated in its own domain — it is still undefeated at route-finding — it simply had no way to enter the domains that came to matter most.

Paradigm 3 · 18 minutes

Learning-based: stop writing rules, start supplying examples

The third answer inverts the problem — and it is the one you will spend the next twenty-five sessions building.

The move is this. You do not know the rule. The expert does not know the rule either, not in words. But you have ten thousand cases where you know the right answer. So: write a program with adjustable numbers inside it, show it the cases, and adjust the numbers until it gets them right.

The inversion — what goes in, and what comes out

Concretely: the spam filter

Nobody sat down and wrote if the message contains "lottery" then it is spam. What happened is duller and far more powerful. The filter was given a million emails, each already labelled spam or not by a human who pressed a button. It then counted.

what counting a million labelled emails produceslearned
# nobody wrote these numbers. they were counted.
word          in spam    in normal mail    evidence
"lottery"       4.1%            0.01%         strong spam
"winner"        3.8%            0.20%         strong spam
"meeting"       0.3%            7.40%         strong normal
"attached"      0.9%            6.10%         normal
"the"           92.0%          93.00%         useless — ignore

Two features of this deserve emphasis. First, the system discovered that "the" is worthless as evidence — nobody told it, it fell out of the counts. Second, and this is the decisive advantage over rules: when spammers change tactics, you do not rewrite anything. You supply newer labelled mail and the numbers move by themselves. A rule-based filter would need a person to notice, diagnose and edit. This one adapts because adaptation is the mechanism.

🔗
You have already seen the machinery

In session 1's setup lab you computed X @ W + b. W is a table of numbers exactly like the one above — evidence weights, one per input. The whole of units 2 and 3 of this course is about how those numbers get set automatically, and you will do it by hand before you do it in code.

What it costs

📊

You need labelled data — a lot of it

And someone has to have labelled it. The 2012 breakthrough you meet in session 3 rested on a dataset of over a million hand-labelled images, which took years to assemble. No data, no learning. This is the binding constraint far more often than algorithms are.

🕳️

You lose the explanation

MYCIN could tell a doctor which clause fired. A trained network has millions of numbers and no story. In medicine, credit and criminal justice this is not a technicality — it can be the reason a working system is not allowed to be deployed.

🪞

It learns what is in the data, not what is true

Including every bias in it. A hiring model trained on ten years of a company's decisions learns that company's history, faithfully. The model is not wrong; it is an accurate mirror, which is worse.

Look at the last row. Rules-based AI scales with a resource that does not grow — human patience and articulacy. The other two scale with resources that have been getting exponentially cheaper for fifty years. That, more than any argument about elegance, is why the field moved.

The closing argument · 6 minutes

Twenty years later, someone played the rematch

The cleanest demonstration that these are competing answers to one question, and not three historical periods.

Chess was search-based AI's crown jewel. In December 2017 DeepMind pointed the third paradigm at it.

AlphaZero was given the rules of chess and nothing else. No opening book, no endgame tables, no grandmaster advice, no hand-designed evaluation features. It played against itself, starting from random moves, and adjusted its own weights based on what won. After roughly four hours of self-play it played a hundred-game match against Stockfish, then the strongest engine in the world and a direct descendant of Deep Blue's approach. AlphaZero won 28, drew 72, and lost none.

The number that should stop you

Stockfish examined about 70 million positions per second. AlphaZero examined about 80 thousand — nearly a thousand times fewer — and won every match it did not draw.

It did not out-search its opponent. It out-judged it. All the effort that Deep Blue's team spent hand-crafting eight thousand evaluation terms, AlphaZero replaced with a learned function that was simply better, and that no human wrote or could read.

This is the whole argument of the course in one result. The same problem, attacked by two paradigms twenty years apart. The hand-written knowledge was excellent — it beat the world champion. The learned knowledge was better, and it was obtained in four hours by a system that started knowing nothing but the rules.

Two honest qualifications, because the story is usually told without them. Those four hours consumed thousands of specialised processors running in parallel, which is not four hours of anything you own. And AlphaZero can play chess, shogi and Go, and cannot configure a VAX, diagnose an infection, or tell you why it did anything at all. Rules-based systems are still deployed today wherever an auditor may ask you to justify a decision.
