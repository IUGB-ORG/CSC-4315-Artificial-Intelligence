Part 1 · 10 minutes

Why a straight line cannot say yes or no

Predicting 1.37 for "did they pass?" is not an answer.

Keep Friday's model and change only the label: y = 1 if the student passed, 0 if not. Fit a line and it will happily predict 1.37 for a student who revised a lot and −0.2 for one who did not. Neither is a probability, and a single very diligent student far to the right drags the whole line flat. We need an output that lives between 0 and 1 and a model that reads as how confident am I that the answer is yes.

Part 2 · 15 minutes

The sigmoid: any number in, a probability out

The first activation function, introduced because we need it.

The neuron, in two lines

z = w·x + b    p = σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

Compute a score exactly as on Friday — a weighted sum plus a bias. Then squash it.

The sigmoid never reaches 0 or 1 — a neuron is never completely certain

Three facts to hold on to. σ(0) = 0.5: a score of zero is a coin toss. The curve is symmetric: σ(−z) = 1 − σ(z). And its slope, which backpropagation will need, is written with its own output: σ′(z) = σ(z)(1 − σ(z)) — largest at 0.25 in the middle and almost zero at the extremes. Remember that last fact; it explains a failure in session 19.

Part 3 · 18 minutes

Cross-entropy: the loss that punishes confident mistakes

And why squared error is the wrong loss for a decision.

Binary cross-entropy

L = −mean( y·log p + (1 − y)·log(1 − p) )

If the label is 1, the loss is −log p: tiny when p is near 1, enormous when p is near 0. If the label is 0, the roles swap. Averaged over the data:

0.693 is a number to remember

It is log 2 — the loss of a model that says 50–50 about everything. A training run that sits at 0.693 has learned nothing. You will see it on Friday, from a neuron that is doing its honest best on a problem it cannot solve.

Why not keep squared error?

Squared error works through a sigmoid, in the sense that the code runs. The trouble is what happens when the neuron is badly wrong. Take a point with label 1 where the neuron says 0.001. The push each loss gives the score z:

The factor that kills learning

∂L/∂z = p − y

Squared error drags in the sigmoid's slope p(1 − p), which is almost zero exactly when the neuron is most wrong. Cross-entropy's log cancels that factor, and the gradient with respect to the score collapses to something beautifully short:

Part 4 · 12 minutes

Training it: Friday's loop, one line different

logistic regression from scratch — two features nowpython
w, b, lr = np.zeros(2), 0.0, 0.5
for epoch in range(500):
    p  = sigmoid(X @ w + b)        # the new line
    dz = p - y                     # from cross-entropy
    w -= lr * X.T @ dz / len(X)    # same shape of update as Friday
    b -= lr * dz.mean()

Put it beside Friday's loop. The update is the same pattern: error times input, averaged, times the learning rate. What changed is one squash in the forward pass and the loss that produced dz. On Lab 8's forty points this reaches 100% training accuracy and a loss of about 0.012.

The decision boundary

The neuron says yes when p > 0.5, which is exactly when z > 0. So the boundary between the two answers is where w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b = 0 — and that is the equation of a straight line. Solve it for x₂ and you can draw it: x₂ = −(w₁x₁ + b)/w₂.

Hold this for Friday

A single neuron, however it is trained, can only ever separate its two classes with a straight line (a flat plane, with more features). Everything it learns is where to put that line. On Friday we meet four points where no line will do.

Three questions

The third is the one that matters for Friday.

Q1. A neuron outputs p = 0.73. What is its score z — positive or negative? Positive: p > 0.5 exactly when z > 0.

Q2. The label is 0 and the neuron says p = 0.9. What is dz? p − y = 0.9. Positive, so the update lowers the score for this point — the right direction.

Q3. Double every weight and the bias. What happens to the boundary? Nothing: w₁x₁ + w₂x₂ + b = 0 has the same solutions after multiplying by 2. What changes is how steep the probabilities are near it — the neuron becomes more confident, not more correct. This is why, on separable data, the weights keep growing forever.
