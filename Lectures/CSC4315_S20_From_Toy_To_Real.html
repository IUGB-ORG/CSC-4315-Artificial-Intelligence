Part 2 · 20 minutes

Three preparation steps, each with a reason

Missing values, categories, scales.

Missing values

A network cannot multiply by NaN. Three honest options: drop the rows (loses 20% of the data for age), drop the column (sensible for deck at 77% missing), or impute — fill each gap with a typical value such as the median. The median of what? That question is the whole session.

Categories

sex is a word. Replacing male with 0 and female with 1 happens to work for two values, but embarked = S, C, Q coded as 0, 1, 2 invents an order and a distance that do not exist. One-hot encoding gives each category its own 0/1 column instead: embarked_S, embarked_C, embarked_Q.

Scales

fare runs into the hundreds while parch is 0 to 6. Session 19 showed what unscaled inputs do to gradient descent. Standardising subtracts each column's mean and divides by its standard deviation, so every feature arrives with mean 0 and spread 1.

🧮
Every one of these steps learns something from data

The imputer learns a median. The scaler learns a mean and a standard deviation. The encoder learns which categories exist. Learning from data is fitting — and anything fitted must only ever see training rows.

Part 3 · 20 minutes · committed on purpose

Data leakage, and a test score that lies

Two leaks: one loud, one quiet.

The loud leak

Use every column as a feature, one-hot encode, split, train a logistic regression, score on the held-out test set:

what the screen showstext
test accuracy: 1.0
A perfect score on real data is a symptom, not a result

Nobody predicts human survival perfectly from a passenger list. Look at the columns again: alive is yes exactly when survived is 1. It is the target, written in words. Target leakage is any feature that would not exist at the moment the prediction is needed — and it always looks like success.

The quiet leak

Now remove alive, but compute the median age and the scaling statistics on all 891 rows before splitting. The test rows have quietly contributed to the numbers used to prepare the training rows. Measured with 10-fold cross-validation:

Order of operationsMean accuracy
preprocess, then split0.7912
split, then preprocess0.7901

A difference of one thousandth. That is the danger: a quiet leak never looks wrong. On a small dataset, with a feature whose statistics drift over time, or with feature selection done on all rows, the same mistake can be worth ten points — and you will not see it until the model meets real users.

The rule

Split first. Then fit every preparation step on the training rows only, and apply the already-fitted steps to the test rows. The only reportable number is the one produced that way, even when the leaky one is barely different.

Part 4 · 12 minutes

A pipeline makes the rule impossible to break

Lab 14's structurepython
prep = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", Pipeline([("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="median")),
                      ("scale",  StandardScaler())]), numeric),
    ("cat", Pipeline([("impute", SimpleImputer(strategy="most_frequent")),
                      ("onehot", OneHotEncoder(handle_unknown="ignore"))]), categorical),
])
pipe = Pipeline([("prep", prep), ("model", LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000))])
pipe.fit(X_train, y_train)            # every median, mean and category learned here — training rows only
print(pipe.score(X_test, y_test))
outputoutput
0.771

77.1% against a baseline of 61.6%: modest, honest, and real. The pipeline object carries every fitted step together, so the same preparation is applied at prediction time and cross-validation refits the whole thing inside each fold — the quiet leak cannot happen.
