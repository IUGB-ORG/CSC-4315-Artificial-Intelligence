Part 1 · 10 minutes

A 98%-accurate model that is useless

A screening programme tests 1,000 people for a condition that 20 of them have. A model answers healthy for every single person. It is right about 980 people: 98% accuracy. It finds none of the 20 people the programme exists to find.

Class imbalance

When one class is rare, a model can achieve high accuracy by ignoring it completely. Always compare to the baseline of predicting the majority class — here, 98% — and never report accuracy alone on imbalanced data.

Part 2 · 20 minutes

The confusion matrix

Four numbers that accuracy squashes into one.

A district health post predicts which of 200 booked patients will miss their appointment, so a nurse can phone them the day before. Treat misses as the positive class:

Read it as the clinic would. When the model flags someone it is usually right (precision 0.8). But it flags almost nobody: 26 of the 30 patients who missed were never phoned. An accuracy of 86.5% sounded like success; a recall of 13% is the truth about what the model does for the clinic.

Part 3 · 20 minutes

The threshold, and the trade-off

Nothing forces the cut-off to be 0.5.

The model outputs a probability. Calling someone a likely no-show when p ≥ 0.5 is a convention, not a law. Move the cut-off and the two error types trade places:

Choose the threshold from what mistakes cost

A phone call costs about 200 FCFA and five minutes of a nurse's time. A missed appointment wastes a consultation slot another patient needed. If a missed slot costs far more than a call, a low threshold — many calls, most no-shows caught — is right even though precision falls. The costs belong to the clinic, not to the model.

ROC and AUC

Sweep the threshold from 1 down to 0 and plot, at each setting, the true-positive rate (recall) against the false-positive rate (share of attending patients wrongly flagged). That curve is the ROC curve. The area under it, AUC, is the probability that the model ranks a randomly chosen no-show above a randomly chosen attender. 0.5 is a coin toss; 1.0 is perfect ranking. This clinic model scores 0.768: it ranks reasonably well, even though the default threshold used that ranking badly.

🧭
Which number to report

AUC judges the ranking, independent of any threshold — good for comparing models. Precision and recall at a chosen threshold judge the decision you will actually deploy — what the clinic lives with. Report both, and always beside the baseline.
