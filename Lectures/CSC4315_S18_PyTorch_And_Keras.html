Part 1 · 10 minutes

Two frameworks, two philosophies

🔥

PyTorch

You write the loop. Tensors, a model made of layers, a loss, an optimiser — and four lines per step that look exactly like yours. Dominant in research.

🧩

Keras

You describe the model and call fit. The loop exists, but inside the library. Runs on TensorFlow (and other back ends) and is popular in production and teaching.

Neither is better in general. PyTorch shows you the loop; Keras hides it. Having written the loop yourself, you can choose deliberately — and read either one.

The zero_grad trap

PyTorch adds new gradients to old ones unless told to forget. Leave out opt.zero_grad() and each step uses the sum of every gradient so far. It runs; it trains badly; nothing warns you.

Part 3 · 15 minutes

Making three frameworks agree exactly

Tuesday's gap came from what we did not control. Today we control everything.

1
Same starting weights. Create them once in NumPy and copy them in. PyTorch stores a layer's weight as (out, in), so it receives W1.T. Keras stores (in, out) — your orientation — so it receives W1 unchanged.
2
Same batches. Draw the shuffles once, as a list of permutations, and let all three loops walk that list.
3
Same arithmetic. float64 everywhere, the same loss, plain SGD with the same learning rate.
What to expect

NumPy and PyTorch agree to around 10⁻¹² — identical arithmetic in a different order. Keras lands within about 10⁻⁶, because its cross-entropy clips probabilities at 10⁻⁷ where yours adds 10⁻¹². A gap of 0.01 means a blank is wrong, most often a missing transpose.

Part 4 · 12 minutes

Autograd: why loss.backward() lands

As PyTorch runs the forward pass, it records every operation in a graph: this matrix multiply fed that addition fed that sigmoid. loss.backward() walks the graph in reverse and applies the chain rule at each node — exactly the product-along-the-path from session 14, for every parameter, automatically. This is automatic differentiation, and it is why nobody derives backward passes for large models by hand.

Why it does not feel like magic today

You have derived the same gradients on the board, coded them in four lines, and proved them with a numerical check. loss.backward() is the function you wrote, generalised. A student who started with PyTorch has to trust it; you can check it — and in session 19 that ability is what finds the bugs.

Three questions

Q1. Which framework needs the transpose, and why? PyTorch: nn.Linear computes x·Wᵀ + b with W stored as (out, in). Our convention is X @ W with W as (in, out).

Q2. What does opt.step() do that your code did in one line? p[k] -= lr * g[k] for every parameter the optimiser was given.

Q3. Keras's curve is 10⁻⁶ away from NumPy's. Is Keras wrong? No — its loss clips probabilities differently. The gap is explained, tiny, and stable. An unexplained gap of any size is what deserves suspicion.
