Part 2 · 10 minutes

Two winters, one cause

Two reports, eight years apart, ended the field's first golden age. Neither of them said the technology did not work.

Machine translation had been promised inside a decade. Russian in, English out, fully automatic. The money followed the promise. By 1966 the Automatic Language Processing Advisory Committee reported back that machine translation was slower than human translation, less accurate than human translation, and more expensive than human translation. Funding for it in the United States effectively stopped.

The deeper problem was one you have already met. In 1973 Sir James Lighthill reviewed the state of UK AI research for the British Science Research Council. His verdict was that in no area had the field produced the impact that had been promised, and his central technical objection was combinatorial explosion — the same wall we walked into on Friday when we computed 10120 for chess.

The argument that closed the labs

Lighthill's case was not that the programs failed. It was that they succeeded on small problems and that the search space grows exponentially, so success on a toy problem tells you nothing about the real one. A technique that handles a three-block world does not scale to a warehouse. It gets exponentially worse.

He was right about search. And he was wrong about the field, because the way out was not a better search algorithm — it was a different paradigm entirely, and it arrived thirty-nine years later.

British AI funding was cut across universities. Researchers left the field or left the country. In the United States, DARPA reduced its support in the same period. The reports gave the sceptics the ammunition; the promises had loaded the gun.

Neither collapse was caused by a technical result. Both were caused by the distance between what had been promised and what had been delivered, and in both cases the promise was made by people who genuinely believed it. Hold on to that when you read a press release this year. The question to ask is not "is this impressive?" but "what exactly was promised, by when, and who is holding the receipt?"

Part 3 · 8 minutes

The uncomfortable fact: the mathematics was already there

The part of this history that surprises people who have only read about 2012.

There is a popular story in which deep learning was invented around 2012 by a small number of people who had a brilliant new idea. Look again at the timeline and count the years.

1943
The artificial neuron
1958
Learning from examples
1986
Backpropagation
1998
Convolutional nets, in production

Backpropagation — the algorithm you will derive by hand in session 14, the algorithm that trains every model in this course and every frontier model in existence — was published in 1986. Twenty-six years before AlexNet.

Convolutional networks were not a promising laboratory idea in 2012. They were a shipped product. LeCun's networks were reading handwritten zip codes at Bell Labs in 1989, were deployed commercially in cheque-reading machines from 1993, and by the late 1990s were reading more than one cheque in ten in the United States — around twenty million cheques a day by 2001. Filters, pooling, a learned hierarchy, trained by backpropagation. Every structural idea in AlexNet was present in LeNet-5.

So ask the obvious question

If the neuron was 1943, the training algorithm was 1986, and working convolutional networks were reading a tenth of America's cheques by 1998 — why did nothing happen until 2012?

The honest answer is not a story about genius. It is a story about arithmetic, and you are going to do the arithmetic yourself in the next fifteen minutes.

Part 4 · 14 minutes · do this in class

Why 2012 and not 1989 — compute it yourself

Four multiplications and one division. No calculus, no code. Just the size of the job and the speed of the machine.

Here are the published facts about training AlexNet in 2012. Nothing here is a secret; it is all in the paper.

QuantityValueWhere it comes from
Operations in one forward pass≈ 1.1 × 109The architecture: 5 convolutional + 3 fully-connected layers
Cost of forward + backward≈ 3 × forwardStandard rule of thumb; you will see why in session 14
Training images1.2 × 106The ImageNet training set, 1000 classes
Passes over the data (epochs)90Reported in the paper

Step 1 — how big is the job?

arithmetic — do it on paper, then check in a notebookpython
work_per_image = 3 * 1.1e9            # forward + backward
total          = work_per_image * 1.2e6 * 90

print(total)     # 3.564e+17  →  about 360 quadrillion operations

Step 2 — how fast was the machine?

AlexNet was trained on two NVIDIA GTX 580 graphics cards, each with 3 GB of memory. Peak throughput of one card is about 1.58 × 1012 operations per second. Real code never reaches peak; assume 30%, which is generous but not absurd.

two GTX 580s, 2012python
speed_2012 = 2 * 1.581e12 * 0.30       # ≈ 9.5e11 ops/second
seconds    = total / speed_2012

print(seconds / 86400)   # 4.3 days
✅
Sanity check — and this is the point of doing it

The paper reports that training took five to six days. You just estimated 4.3 days from four published numbers and one assumption. Landing that close means the model of "size of job ÷ speed of machine" is capturing something real. Now we can trust it for the counterfactual.

Step 3 — the counterfactual

Now run the same job on the hardware that was available when backpropagation was published and LeCun's networks were being built. A good scientific workstation of that era ran on the order of 10 million operations per second.

Before you compute it — write down your guess

How long would that same training run have taken in 1989? Commit to a number. Most people are wrong by a factor of a hundred.

3.564e17 / 1e7 = 3.56e10 seconds

Divide by the number of seconds in a year (about 3.16 × 107):

≈ 1,130 years.

Start the job in 1989 and it finishes in the year 3119. Even on a machine ten times faster than the one assumed, it is still 113 years. There is no amount of cleverness, funding or persistence that closes a gap of that size.

This is the answer to "why 2012 and not 1989". Not a missing idea. Not a lack of talent. The job was simply, arithmetically, impossible to run — and the people trying were not failing, they were early.

And notice where the hardware came from

Nobody built the GTX 580 for artificial intelligence. It was built to render video games. It happens to do the one operation a neural network layer needs — the same multiply-and-add, over and over, on thousands of numbers at once. The field's decisive instrument was a side effect of the games industry. In session 7 you will write that operation as a single line of NumPy and see exactly why a graphics card is the right shape for it.

The second gift: the data

Compute was half of it. The other half was ImageNet — 1.2 million labelled training images across a thousand categories, assembled over years by people paying human beings to label pictures. Backpropagation with no data teaches a network nothing. In 1989 the data did not exist either, and building it was a logistical project, not a research one.

The hinge of this session

Everything up to here is history. Everything after it is the present — and it is the same story. What made 2012 possible was not a cleverer idea but more data and more compute, and what determines who benefits in 2026 is still, largely, who has data and who has compute. Keep that in your head for the next forty minutes.

Part 5 · 8 minutes

What actually works, in September 2026

Not what is promised. Not what is demonstrated on stage. What you can rely on next week.

I am going to avoid naming a best model, and you should understand why rather than assume I am dodging. Any ranking I give you today will be wrong by December — these systems are replaced on a timescale of months. What does not go stale is the shape of the capability. So: three tiers.

✅

RELIABLE

Transcribing speech in a well-resourced language. Translating between well-resourced languages. Classifying images into known categories. Drafting, summarising and rewriting text. Writing routine code and explaining unfamiliar code. Extracting structured fields from documents.

⚠️

WORKS WITH SUPERVISION

Multi-step reasoning. Anything numerical. Any claim of fact, citation or reference. Long agentic tasks with several tools. Code touching an unfamiliar codebase. All of this is useful and none of it is trustworthy unattended.

❌

DOES NOT WORK

Knowing what it does not know. Reliable arithmetic without a tool. Genuine novelty outside the training distribution. Any language with little text on the internet. Guarantees of any kind.

The most useful single sentence about these systems

They are excellent at tasks where plausible and correct coincide, and dangerous exactly where those two come apart.

Writing a paragraph in the style of a report: plausible is correct. Citing the paper that paragraph refers to: plausible is not correct, and the system cannot tell the difference — because nothing in its training taught it to represent that difference. Session 27 explains why in mechanical terms. For now, use the sentence as a filter.

Part 6 · 10 minutes

How to read a leaderboard

This is a skill, and it will still be worth something in ten years. A ranking is worth about six weeks.

You will be shown leaderboards constantly — in vendor pitches, in the press, in papers, by colleagues arguing about which model to adopt. Four things go wrong with them, and once you can name all four you will read them better than most people who cite them.

Problem 1 — Saturation

When every frontier model scores above 88% on a benchmark, the remaining differences are inside the noise. MMLU, for years the most-cited general capability test, is functionally saturated: the gap between first and fourth place at the top of that board tells you nothing you can act on. GSM8K, the grade-school maths benchmark, is now answered at around 99% by frontier systems, which makes it useful only for evaluating small models — it can no longer distinguish large ones.

A saturated benchmark does not stop being published. It stops being informative, which is worse, because the number still looks like information.

Problem 2 — Contamination

A benchmark is a fixed set of questions published on the internet. Models are trained on the internet. You can see the difficulty.

If the test items are in the training data, the score measures recall rather than reasoning — and it collapses the moment you hand the model a fresh question of the same type. This is not hypothetical or rare. Training corpora are now so large that the labs themselves have limited certainty about what is inside them.

📉
A concrete example worth remembering

In February 2026, OpenAI's evaluations team announced it would stop reporting SWE-bench Verified — for three years the unofficial scoreboard for whether a model can write real software. Not because their score was bad. Because contamination had made the number stop meaning anything. When the organisation with the strongest incentive to cite a favourable benchmark walks away from it, that is worth more than any single score on it.

Problems 3 and 4 — who built it, and the column nobody reads

Ask who assembled the board. A vendor's own comparison selects the benchmarks on which that vendor wins, and does so without lying about a single number. An independent board is better, but someone still chose which tasks count as "capability", and that choice is an argument rather than a measurement.

Then look for the column that is almost never in the headline: cost, latency, and whether the thing can run where you need it to run. A model two points better and forty times more expensive is the worse choice for nearly every real deployment, and a model you cannot run offline is no help at all in a field where the network is unreliable. The ranking answers a question you were not asking.

Do this now — five minutes

A vendor sends you this table to justify adopting Model A. Find as many problems as you can before opening the answer.

ModelMMLUGSM8KArena rankCost / 1M tokens
Model A (ours)91.298.7#1$18.00
Model B90.498.1#3$2.50
Model C89.899.0#2$0.80
How many did you find?

There are at least six. Write your list first.

1. Both benchmarks are saturated. All three models are above 88% on MMLU and above 98% on GSM8K. The 0.8-point MMLU spread between A and B is not a capability difference you can act on; it is noise dressed as a decimal.

2. Both benchmarks are public and static. Contamination is not merely possible, it should be your default assumption. These numbers partly measure memory.

3. Model C beats Model A on GSM8K — and the table is arranged so you read left to right and stop. Always read every column, including the ones that do not flatter the pitch.

4. The arena rank has no confidence interval. Rank #1 and rank #3 in a preference arena routinely sit inside overlapping intervals, which means the ordering may not be statistically real at all. Ask for the interval; if it is not offered, the rank is decoration.

5. Model A costs 22.5× Model C. For a 1.4-point MMLU difference that is inside the noise. On any workload with volume, this is the only column that matters.

6. The vendor made the table. No harness is stated, no evaluation date, no methodology, and the benchmarks chosen are the ones their model wins. Note also what is absent: nothing here tests your actual task.

The professional answer is not "Model C". It is: none of this tells me anything. Give me two hundred examples of my own task and I will evaluate all three myself. That is what session 21 teaches you to do properly.

Part 7 · 8 minutes

The gap that is on no leaderboard

Everything above was about how well these systems work. Now: for whom.

Africa has more than two thousand languages. A 2025 survey of six large language models, eight smaller multilingual models and six African-specialised models found roughly forty-two African languages supported in total — and only four appearing consistently across models: Amharic, Swahili, Afrikaans and Malagasy. That leaves over 98% of African languages with essentially no support. Twenty writing systems in active use are not handled at all.

And where support exists, it is thinner than the marketing suggests. On IrokoBench, a benchmark built specifically for African languages, the best model tested showed a performance gap of up to 28 points against English and up to 19 points against French. Not a rounding difference — a different product.

🗣️
This is not an abstract concern in this room

In one large study, Bambara — a Mande language, closely related to the Dioula spoken widely across this country — was among the languages whose model performance correlated least with every other language tested. The researchers attributed this to Mande grammar differing sharply from the language families that dominate training data. Techniques that transfer well between European languages do not transfer here. The problem is not that nobody got around to Dioula. It is structural.

Why this happens, and why it is the same story as session 3

On Tuesday we established that the 2012 breakthrough waited twenty-six years for compute and labelled data, and that both arrived from outside the field. The language gap is the identical mechanism, running now.

Training a frontier model needs trillions of tokens of text and a compute budget in the tens to hundreds of millions of dollars. A language with little text on the internet cannot supply the first, and most institutions cannot supply the second. Even tokenisation works against you: the tokenizers are fitted to high-resource languages, so a low-resource language is chopped into more pieces, costs more per sentence, and fits worse in a context window. The disadvantage compounds at every layer.

The consequence, stated without comfort

If the binding constraint on this field is infrastructure rather than insight, then whoever builds the infrastructure decides what gets built. Nobody is going to assemble a Dioula corpus on your behalf.

That is not a complaint. It is a description of an opening — the kind that, in 2009, was called "a labelled image dataset nobody wants to build."

Closing · 6 minutes

Côte d'Ivoire, and why this course exists

The national position, the ambition, and the honest distance between them.

In the 2023 Oxford Insights Government AI Readiness Index, Côte d'Ivoire ranked 138th of 193 countries. That assessment is part of what produced the response.

In March 2025 the Ministry of Digital Transition and Digitalisation, with support from the World Bank, formalised the Stratégie Nationale de l'Intelligence Artificielle — SNIA 2030. Its stated aim is to position Côte d'Ivoire as a regional leader in AI adoption in West Africa by 2030.

Read the pillars against what you learned today

Investment answers the compute constraint. Inclusion answers the language and data constraint. Governance answers the deployment constraint that killed MYCIN — a system that worked and was never used, because nobody had resolved who is responsible when it is wrong. The three pillars are not slogans; each one names a real bottleneck you have now met by its technical name.

Now the honest part. A strategy is a document. 138th place is a measurement. The distance between them is closed by people who can build things, and that is a smaller group than the country needs. There are twenty-six sessions left in this course, and at the end of them you will have written a neural network from nothing, trained it, and built a small language model. That is not a complete answer to a national strategy. It is, however, exactly the kind of person the strategy is short of.

Why this course is taught the way it is

You could learn to call an API in an afternoon, and thousands of people here already have. That skill is real and it is also common, and it leaves you dependent on infrastructure built elsewhere for problems defined elsewhere.

Understanding what is inside the box is what lets you build one for a problem nobody else has bothered with. That is why sessions 9 to 14 have no libraries in them.
