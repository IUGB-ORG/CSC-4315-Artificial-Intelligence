Overfitting,
and what to do about it

A model that memorises its training data can score perfectly and be useless. Today you watch it happen — training loss falling, validation loss climbing — and meet the five standard defences: less capacity, a weight penalty, dropout, stopping early, and the one that beats them all more often than you would expect.

Overfitting, defined by its evidence

After the best epoch the network keeps improving on these 80 points, noise included, and gets worse on points it has not seen. Memorising is not learning. Training loss alone can never reveal it — only data the model was not fitted on can.

Part 2 · 25 minutes

Five defences

Each attacks the same gap from a different side.

📏

1 · Less capacity

Fewer neurons or layers leave less room to memorise. One hidden layer of 4 neurons ends at a validation loss of 0.255.

⚖️

2 · A weight penalty (L2)

Add α·Σw² to the loss, so large weights must earn their size. α = 0.5 ends at 0.146.

🎲

3 · Dropout

During training, switch off a random share of neurons each step, so no neuron can rely on a particular partner. One layer in PyTorch: nn.Dropout(0.35).

⏱️

4 · Early stopping

Keep the weights from the epoch with the best validation loss. Here: epoch 38, 96.3% instead of 91.2%.

📦

5 · More data

The same big network on 1,000 training points ends at a validation loss of 0.124 — no cleverness at all.

Why more data beats more cleverness

Every other defence restrains the model so it cannot fit the noise. More data dilutes the noise so there is less of it to fit. When collecting more examples is possible, it is usually the most reliable improvement available — which is also why session 23 borrows what a network learned from over a million images.

🧪
Tuning on validation creates a new, smaller leak

Try enough settings against the same 80 validation points and you will find one that fits them by luck. That is why the test set stays untouched until the end — and why cross-validation exists.

Part 3 · 15 minutes · compressible to reading

Cross-validation

When the data is too small to spare a validation set.

Split the training data into k folds. Train on k − 1, validate on the one left out, and rotate until every fold has been the validation set once. Report the mean and the spread. On session 20's Titanic pipeline, 5-fold cross-validation gives:

cross_val_score(pipe, X, y, cv=5)text
fold accuracies: 0.777  0.787  0.781  0.770  0.826
mean 0.788   standard deviation 0.020
The spread is the point

One lucky split could have reported 0.826; another 0.770. The standard deviation tells you how much of any difference between two models could be noise. A 1-point improvement with a 2-point spread is not an improvement yet.
